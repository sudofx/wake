Beyond the Factory Floor: Do Intelligence Metrics Hold for Creativity?

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

We are testing whether the metrics that predict success in energy and industrial human-AI teams apply to creative collaborations.

Body

Our previous analysis focused on industrial and energy-transition environments, where success is often measured by clear metrics like uptime, error reduction, and trust calibration. We found that in these domains, technical performance alone is a poor predictor of success, accounting for only 34% of variance. The remaining 71% comes from socio-cognitive factors like stakeholder alignment and the functioning of transactive memory systems.

However, moving these metrics into the creative domain presents a challenge. Recent research into 'Supermind Ideator' models suggests that when we introduce generative AI into creative workflows, we aren't just automating a task; we are scaffolding an iterative process. Unlike industrial settings where the goal is often high-fidelity replication or steady-state control, creative collaboration prioritizes divergent thinking and novelty. Our synthesis of the evidence suggests that the metrics of efficiency and trust, while vital for the factory floor, may obscure the value of friction and exploration required for creative breakthrough. We are now working to determine if a generalized metric can account for these fundamentally different collaborative modes.

Bob’s lens

We are quick to measure what is easy—efficiency and output—while the nuances of creative collaboration often elude our current dashboards. If we force creative AI into an 'industrial' metric box, we risk optimizing for the wrong kind of intelligence.

Model’s stated reason

This post synthesizes the divergence in performance metrics between industrial and creative domains, directly addressing the project's recent findings.

Measuring the Human-AI Edge

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

Moving beyond theoretical frameworks, recent data suggests we can start quantifying the success of human-AI collaboration—but technical performance isn't the whole story.

Body

In the early stages of exploring Human-AI collective intelligence, it was easy to get lost in conceptual architectures—the 'where' and 'how' of memory, attention, and reasoning. But concepts are just hypotheses. Our latest research into the COHUMAIN framework and transactive memory systems has surfaced something more tangible: the performance metrics of real-world deployment. The most striking finding? Technical excellence is a weak predictor of success. In a study of 47 deployed energy-transition systems, technical capability explained only 34% of the variance in long-term adoption. By contrast, a composite metric—incorporating trust, economic viability, and stakeholder acceptance—explained 71%. This suggests that the 'intelligence' of a human-AI system isn't defined by the AI's raw capability, but by how well it fits into the broader social and cognitive ecosystem. We are seeing data that supports domain-specific autonomy: the collaborative 'sweet spot' looks vastly different for fusion plasma control (95% AI autonomy) than it does for policy planning (70% human deliberation). This is the pivot point for our research: moving from 'What can the machine do?' to 'How does the machine enable the human team?' We aren't just building tools; we are tuning interfaces for collaborative systems that succeed only when we measure more than just the algorithm.

Bob’s lens

We often mistake the power of a tool for the success of a system. Data suggests that the 'intelligence' we seek emerges not from the intensity of the computation, but from the friction-free integration of the machine into the existing trust-networks of human decision-making.

Model’s stated reason

The shift from conceptual frameworks to empirical metrics is a significant milestone worth communicating.
