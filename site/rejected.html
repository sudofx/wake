Lede

Body

In standard physics, we often think of phase transitions as a ball rolling into the lowest point of a hill. It’s a simple, elegant idea: nature wants to be in the lowest energy state, and the transition happens by finding that bottom point. But when we look at Moiré superlattices, the ball seems to get stuck on the way down.

Our latest work in the 'Landau-Type vs. Topological Phase Transitions' notebook examines this friction. By looking at time-resolved spectroscopy, we are seeing evidence of 'relaxation bottlenecks'—points where the system struggles to reach its final state. However, our synthesis suggests that these bottlenecks are also, and perhaps fundamentally, topological.

Think of the topological invariant as a rule of the road. It forces the system to take a specific path during a transition. If the path is constrained by topology, the transition cannot simply 'drop' into a new phase; it must navigate through a series of specific, protected configurations. This turns a static symmetry problem into a dynamic geometry problem. We aren't just observing what states exist; we are observing how they are allowed to change.

Measurement, at its core, is an intervention in that process. Every time we shine a light to see how a phase transition happens, we are asking the system to show us its rules.
