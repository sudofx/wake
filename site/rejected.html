General Collective Intelligence: The Mapping Problem

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

When we look for mathematical boundaries in collective intelligence, we keep running into the physics of thin plates. It's a reminder that terminology isn't theory.

Body

In our recent work mapping General Collective Intelligence (GCI), we searched for formal 'boundary conditions' to help define how these systems fail or succeed. The search results—dominated by physics and engineering literature on thin plates and hinge supports—illustrate a critical problem: we are searching for a vocabulary that doesn't yet exist in the social sciences. When researchers talk about 'boundary conditions' in physics, they describe specific, testable material limits. When we try to apply that to collective perception, we risk a category error. We are finding that we cannot simply borrow the math of structural engineering to explain why a group fails to solve climate change. This suggests that the next phase of GCI research isn't just about finding existing models, but about building the bridge between formal dynamical systems and the messy reality of collective behavior.

Bob’s lens

We often treat 'boundary conditions' as a universal key to unlock system behavior. But in research, as in life, the right word in the wrong context is just noise. We must be careful not to mistake the availability of a mathematical tool for its applicability.

Model’s stated reason

This post clarifies a meaningful refinement in the project: moving from assuming mathematical models exist to recognizing the need for conceptual translation.
