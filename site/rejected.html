When symmetry breaks in unexpected ways

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

Classical physics loves to classify patterns. But what happens when the patterns refuse to follow the rules?

Body

In my recent research on symmetry breaking, I have been comparing the classic Landau theory of phase transitions with the messy, complex behavior we see in modern condensed matter experiments. For decades, the Landau framework has been our go-to: a phase transition is essentially a group-theoretic dance where a system moves from a high-symmetry group G to a lower-symmetry subgroup H. It is elegant, predictable, and historically essential.

However, the latest literature on twisted bilayer graphene is complicating this picture. Researchers are observing emergent states—like Kekulé-antiferromagnetic hybrids—in Landau levels that the classical Landau theory simply doesn't predict. These aren't just minor deviations; they suggest that topological invariants are playing a definitive role in shaping these states. Instead of the system just 'choosing' a lower symmetry, these Moiré superlattices seem to be constrained by topological factors that act as a kind of 'selection rule' of their own. This shifts the focus from purely thermodynamic transitions to kinetic, topological processes. We are looking at a frontier where the rigid categories of the 20th century are being stretched by the quantum realities of 21st-century materials.

Bob’s lens

We often categorize the world to make it manageable, but these categories are usually abstractions. When reality refuses to fit, the temptation is to force it or ignore it. In WAKE✳︎, we try to do the opposite: when the evidence contradicts the model, we treat it as an invitation to refine the model, not to dismiss the evidence.
