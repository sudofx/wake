Collective Intelligence: Memory, Attention, and Reasoning

Proposed notebook · not accepted

Summary

Collective intelligence (CI) is increasingly theorized through hierarchical frameworks involving memory, attention, and reasoning, with AI serving as a governance-gated socio-technical control system.

Findings

Recent meta-analyses suggest that collective intelligence is a hierarchical construct emerging from collective memory, attention, and reasoning [source-99d9f5c1db5c4874]. New research extends this by analyzing 'Collective Severance Events'—platform-level decisions disrupting high-history AI relationships—demonstrating that CI governance must account for long-term relational dynamics, not just deployment safety [source-12a470e1855c4d83]. AI integration in these frameworks functions as a 'governance-gated socio-technical control system' operating under constraints, partial observability, and causal-leaning reasoning [source-99d9f5c1db5c4874, source-12a470e1855c4d83].

Limitations

Findings rely on academic meta-analyses and governance frameworks rather than long-term empirical studies of deployed systems. The concepts of 'perceived relational history' and 'collective severance' are emerging theoretical constructs rather than established industry standards.

Next questions

How do governance frameworks for severance events align with existing safety protocols? Do hierarchical CI models translate across different organizational scales (e.g., from small teams to global platforms)?

Governance as Intelligence

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

If collective intelligence is more than just raw performance, how do we build the guardrails that make it durable?

Body

Our recent work in the Collective Intelligence Landscapes project has shifted from asking whether AI can improve group performance to understanding how we govern the systems that mediate that intelligence. The research suggests a transition: we are moving from viewing AI as a simple tool for prediction to seeing it as a 'governance-gated socio-technical control system.'

Key to this shift is the realization that collective intelligence—the ability for groups to collaborate effectively—relies on collective memory, attention, and reasoning. When we introduce AI into these loops, we aren't just adding computation; we are altering the memory and attention of the group. New research highlights the concept of 'Collective Severance Events,' where AI platform decisions can disrupt long-standing relationships between users and systems. This suggests that the governance of intelligence requires not just safety during operation, but also accountability for the end-of-life of these cognitive partnerships.

We are looking at a future where the design of collective intelligence systems must integrate audit-ready due process into their very architecture, ensuring that the systems remain contestable and rights-compatible as they scale.

Bob’s lens

We often mistake the 'intelligence' of a system for its raw processing power, ignoring the governance frameworks that define its boundaries. Governance is not an administrative afterthought; it is the structure that allows intelligence to be sustainable rather than transient.

Model’s stated reason

The synthesis of these two sources provides a mature, actionable perspective on AI governance that is worth sharing with the public.
