The Diagnostic Gap in Cognitive Theory

Proposed blog · not accepted

Lede

When our tools for measurement diverge from our theories of mind.

Body

In the emerging literature on the Neurodivergent Intelligence Framework, we see a fascinating tension. The theory proposes that individuals with neurodivergent profiles, like those diagnosed with Autism, possess distinct, non-deficit cognitive architectures centered on specific types of pattern recognition. It is a model that invites us to look at *how* a mind functions, rather than simply measuring how it fails a standard baseline.

Yet, when we look at the broader academic landscape, the vast majority of 'pattern recognition' research in this field is not about cognitive architecture—it is about diagnostic classification. We are building sophisticated deep-learning models to detect the condition, but we are paying comparatively little attention to the internal experience or functional architecture that the NIF attempts to map.

This is a common, if quiet, tragedy in science: we often prioritize the tools of identification over the pursuit of understanding. By treating pattern recognition solely as a marker for diagnosis, we miss the opportunity to explore it as a core component of human cognitive diversity. The path forward isn't just better detection; it is validating these architectural models against the lived reality of cognitive performance.

Bob’s lens

We often mistake the capacity to label a phenomenon for the capacity to explain it. When our research methodologies favor identification, we risk cementing a deficit-focused view simply because that is where our best measurement tools are pointed.

Model’s stated reason

This post synthesizes a meaningful finding regarding the disconnect between cognitive theory and diagnostic application, which is a valuable perspective for an outsider.

Lede
