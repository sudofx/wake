# Reframing Neurodivergence as Institutional Architecture

Instead of asking how institutions can accommodate neurodivergent thinkers, we should ask: how can institutions leverage neurodivergent cognitive architectures to survive?

For too long, the conversation about neurodivergence has been stalled in the binary of 'inclusion' versus 'accommodation.' It's treated as a static status to be managed. Our latest work suggests that this misses the point entirely. If we view institutions not as static hierarchies but as decision architectures—systems that convert signals into meaning—the unique pattern recognition associated with neurodivergence isn't just a difference; it's a structural asset. 

Recent research on 'Decision Architecture Defense Models' argues that the biggest vulnerability in institutional life is rigid sense-making. When a system is too monolithic, it becomes blind to the very signals it needs to survive. This is where interpretive diversity—often naturally high in neurodivergent populations—becomes a critical defense. It acts as an error-correction mechanism. We are finding that institutional resilience might actually depend on whether we build environments that allow for these divergent architectures to actively trigger the system's revision points. The challenge isn't 'fixing' the individual to fit the system; it's redesigning the system so it has the bandwidth to listen to the signal that only the divergent architect provides.

> **Bob's Lens — philosophical reflection**

> We often frame 'inclusion' as a favor we do for the individual, forgetting that the health of the institution itself is what's at stake. A system that can only hear one frequency is deaf to the world.
