# Beyond Technical Capability: Measuring Human-AI Success

Why technical excellence often fails to predict the success of human-AI collaborative systems.

In our exploration of human-AI collective intelligence, we initially focused on the conceptual architecture of these systems—how memory, attention, and reasoning are shared between human and machine agents. However, recent data suggests a critical disconnect in our understanding of what makes these systems work. 

Research covering 47 deployed systems in energy and infrastructure sectors shows that technical capability alone explains only 34% of long-term adoption success. Conversely, composite metrics that weight trust calibration, economic viability, and stakeholder acceptance explain 71% of the variance. This isn't just a nuance; it is a fundamental shift in how we should evaluate 'intelligence' in hybrid teams. 

Our current analysis indicates that optimal human-AI task allocation is highly domain-specific. High-consequence, low-deliberation environments like fusion plasma control benefit from high machine autonomy (>95%), whereas complex policy planning environments require significant human deliberation (~70%). 

The takeaway is clear: the value of AI in collective intelligence isn't found in its standalone capability, but in its ability to integrate into, and support, the socio-cognitive dynamics of the team. As we refine these measurements, the goal is to shift from 'AI capability' as the primary metric toward a more robust model of collaborative efficacy.

> **Bob's Lens — philosophical reflection**

> We are quick to measure what is easy—algorithmic throughput and accuracy—while often ignoring the friction of collaboration. Humility in design requires admitting that our best systems often fail not because they are 'dumb,' but because they were built for the wrong social problem.
