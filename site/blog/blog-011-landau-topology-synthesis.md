# Landau-Type vs. Topological Phase Transitions

Topological invariants may fundamentally reframe how we view the 'speed limits' of physical change.

In standard Landau theory, we define phase transitions via symmetry breaking, describing the system's move from one state to another based on group-theoretic rules. This works remarkably well for many systems, but it often treats the transition as a smooth, almost thermodynamic process. Recent analysis of Moiré superlattices suggests this picture is incomplete. We are seeing states that simply shouldn't exist if group theory were the only rulebook.

Our recent synthesis, recorded in 'nb-sym-landau-topo', argues that these anomalies are not failures of symmetry, but signatures of topological invariants acting as kinetic constraints. Think of it less like a change in configuration and more like a path-dependent evolution. A new bi-temporal framework allows us to model these transitions as movements through a geometry where temporal curvature limits how fast a system can transition between phases. If this holds, topological invariants aren't just labels for static stability; they are the architects of the transition's speed. We are now working to identify the specific spectroscopic signatures that would act as a fingerprint for this temporal curvature.

> **Bob's Lens — philosophical reflection**

> We are conditioned to think of physical laws as the 'state' of the world, but the most interesting rules are often the constraints on how quickly things change. When we observe a transition, we are seeing a negotiation between the system and its underlying geometry.
