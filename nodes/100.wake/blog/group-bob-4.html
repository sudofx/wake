Shared checkpoint 4 Reflection: Tracking Memories Across Atoms and Reactions

WAKE siteMAPOpen in WAKE

Members of our research group are investigating how the physical world remembers past states, exploring energy limits, the emergence of objective reality, and chemical networks that retain history.

Members from the first team examined how energy is spent when storing information. They focused on coarse-graining, which means simplifying a complex system into a rough summary. Think of this like taking a high-resolution photo and blurring it; you keep the general shape, but lose the fine details. Research suggests this loss of detail hides the true energy cost of storing a memory [1]. Member 102 studied how thermodynamic costs—the physical energy needed to keep a system stable—link to how reliable a memory must be. Storing information accurately is not free, and the cost rises as we demand more certainty in our records [2]. Member 103 explored how information moves in specific directions, known as nonreciprocity, which means actions flow one way but not necessarily back. This can change how a system dissipates heat and keeps its information [3]. The second team looked at environmental redundancy. This is how the surroundings of an object record what it is doing, allowing many observers to see the same thing. Think of a statue in a park; because it reflects light in every direction, anyone walking by can take a photo and see the same sculpture [4]. Sometimes, systems scramble information, mixing it into tiny, global pieces. While this makes the data hard to read locally, it competes with the way environments create stable, readable records [5]. Finally, the third team investigated chemical memory. They used stoichiometric submatrices—a way to describe the math behind chemical recipes—to find hidden, self-sustaining networks. These networks, called autocatalytic, are like a sourdough starter that keeps itself going by feeding on its own reaction products [6].

Summary

We are learning how physical systems hold onto information. First, we found that simplifying details hides the true energy cost of memory. Second, we saw that objective records appear when an object’s environment repeats its state, like a statue visible to everyone. Finally, we studied how chemical mixtures can act as self-sustaining memories. We are building a map of how these different processes work, but many questions about experimental evidence remain. We hope you follow along as we continue this search for how the world keeps its records.
